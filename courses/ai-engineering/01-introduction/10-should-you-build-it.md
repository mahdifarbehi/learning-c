# Should you build it?

@concept
## Demo easy, product hard
If you just want to learn, build anything. Building is one of the best ways to learn, and many AI leaders told their teams to experiment.

But if it's your job, step back first. With foundation models, a cool demo is easy. A profitable product is hard.
---
@concept
## Why build this at all?
The first question is **why**. An AI app is usually a response to a risk or an opportunity.

From highest risk to lowest, there are three common reasons. The next three cards cover them.
---
@concept
## Reason 1: or a competitor wipes you out
If AI is a serious threat to your business, adopting it comes first.

In Gartner's 2023 survey, 7% of executives gave this reason. It is common where the work is processing documents and gathering information, like financial analysis, insurance and data processing. It's also common in creative work: advertising, web design, image production.
---
@concept
## Reason 2: or you miss profit
Most companies adopt AI for the opportunity.

AI can make getting users cheaper, with better ad copy, product descriptions and visuals. It can keep users longer, with better support and personalization. It also helps with sales leads, internal communication, market research and watching competitors.
---
@concept
## Reason 3: or you fall behind
You don't know yet where AI fits, but you don't want to be left behind.

A company shouldn't chase every hype. But many failed by waiting too long: think Kodak, Blockbuster and BlackBerry.

If you can afford it, exploring a big new technology is sensible. At large companies, this can be R&D work. Small startups usually can't spare even one person to look around.
---
@concept
## Build it or buy it?
Once you have a good reason, ask whether you must build it yourself.

If AI threatens your core business, keep that work in-house rather than depend on a competitor.

If AI is just there to boost profit and productivity, buying a ready-made tool often saves time and money and may work better.
---
@concept
## AI's role: critical or complementary
Apple describes several ways AI can fit into a product. Three matter here. The first:

**Complementary**: the app still works without AI. Gmail works without Smart Compose.

**Critical**: the app fails without AI. Face ID doesn't work without face recognition.

The more critical AI is, the more accurate and reliable it must be. People forgive mistakes when AI isn't the core.
---
@concept
## Reactive or proactive
**Reactive**: AI answers when the user asks. A chatbot is reactive. It usually needs to be fast.

**Proactive**: AI shows something when it sees a chance. Traffic alerts on Google Maps are proactive. They can be computed ahead of time, so speed matters less.

But users didn't ask for proactive output, so low quality feels intrusive. Proactive features need a higher quality bar.
---
@concept
## Dynamic or static
**Dynamic**: updated all the time from user feedback. Face ID must keep up as your face changes. With AI, this can mean a model per user, or a memory feature like ChatGPT's that remembers your preferences.

**Static**: updated now and then. Object detection in Google Photos probably changes only when the app is upgraded, with one shared model for many users.
---
@concept
## The human's role
Also decide what humans do. For a support chatbot, AI could:

Suggest replies that human agents use to answer faster.

Answer simple requests itself and pass hard ones to people.

Answer every request alone.

Keeping humans involved in AI's decisions is called **human-in-the-loop**.
---
@concept
## Crawl, walk, run
Microsoft (2023) proposed raising automation step by step:
```diagram
crawl: a human must be involved
↓
walk: AI talks directly to employees
↓
run: AI talks directly to customers
```
Move up as quality is proven. If agents use 95% of AI's suggested replies to simple requests word for word, you can let customers talk to AI directly for those requests.
